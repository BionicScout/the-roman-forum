## Description
There are two competing ideas of on sin. The first is [[All sin separates you from God's grace]] which means that no matter what the sin is, you are separated from God. While this idea is good, because we have to get our sin forgiven, it also can lead to the thinking of "well murder is just as bad lying so murder is fine because I can be forgiven". That thinking is horrible cause it lead to bad things. 

The second idea is [[Some sins are greater in severity than other sins]]. You can see throughout the bible in numerous places. This idea avoids the trap of not doing worse sins because they are bad, but the make the doing the lesser sins more easy to do as they are "not that bad in comparison". 

To avoid the trap of both of these ideas, we need to combine them. By stating that we are covered in guilt by doing any amount of sin, we allow all sin to have an initial cost. But by also saying that separation gets farther the more sever the sin is, then we have allow for people to avoid greater sins with more effort. This balance allows the positives of both and avoids the negatives as long as you emphasize that you should always avoid sin but only make big deals about greater sins. 

This form of teaching can be seen inside the Catholic Church. [[Aurelius Augustine|St. Augustine]] states in his [[Tractates on the Gospel of John]] that "crime is grievous sin, deserving in the highest measure to be denounced and condemned. Not, however, that God condemns certain sins, and justifies and praises certain others. He approves of none. He hates them all" (Tractate 41, §9). This both shows that there are grievous sins called crimes but also showing that God hates all sin. This strikes a good line between both ideas. [[Thomas Aquinas|St. Thomas Aquinas]] later shows a very similar idea in his [[Summa Theologica]] where he states that sin in "a sickness of the soul" (I-II, Q. 88, Art. 1), while later defining mortal sins which  are "irreparable defect consisting in the corruption of a principle" (I-II, Q. 88, Art. 1), and venial sins which are "sins which imply a disorder in things referred to the end, the order to the end itself being preserved, are reparable" (I-II, Q. 88, Art. 1).
